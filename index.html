Field guide · robots in unstructured places

The Sim2Real Gap

A simulator is a hypothesis about the world. A policy trained inside it learns the hypothesis, including the parts that are wrong. The gap is everything the hypothesis leaves out. It is why a robot can succeed every time on screen and only most of the time on site.

Blue is always the simulatorOrange is always the real site

The short version

  • The gap is a mismatch between the worlds you simulated and the world you deploy into. Wrong numbers can be measured or randomized. Missing physics cannot be fixed by any setting.
  • Simulators handle rigid-body motion well. Most of the gap sits in what they leave out unless you add it: actuator limits, sensor noise, calibration error, delay and wear.
  • Small gaps compound. Millimetres stack against tolerances, milliseconds destabilize fast loops, and a 2% shortfall per step halves a 30-step task.
  • Randomization buys coverage and costs sharpness. Adaptation, real data and forgiving hardware recover the rest.
  • Repeatability belongs to the fleet, not the robot. Failures cluster by unit, site and day, and a success rate needs hundreds of trials before it means much.

Section 1

One world in training, another on site

Training in simulation means sampling worlds from a distribution someone wrote down: masses, friction values, camera models, object sets, lighting. The policy becomes good at that distribution. Deployment samples worlds from the real distribution, which nobody wrote down. The sim2real gap is the difference between the two, as the policy experiences it.

The mismatch comes in two kinds, and they need different fixes.

Parameter error

The simulator has the right physics with the wrong numbers. Friction is set to 0.8 where the part is oily and the real value is 0.3. Latency is zero where the real loop takes 40 ms. Fix it by measuring the real value, or by training across a range that includes it.

Model error

The simulator is missing the physics. Soil that compacts, a hose that whips, a dusty lens, a gearbox with backlash. No setting of the existing knobs reproduces it. Fix it by adding a model, learning one from real data, or making the robot indifferent to it.

Field note · Peng et al. 2018

One puck-pushing task on a Fetch arm randomized 95 dynamics parameters: the mass of each link, the damping of each joint, the puck’s mass, friction and damping, the table height, controller gains, the time between actions, and observation noise.

One slider understates the problem. A real robot has dozens of uncertain parameters, and the policy needs all of them inside its training ranges at the same time.

If each of 20 uncertain parameters lands inside its training range 95% of the time, all of them land inside together only 36% of the time.

Assumes the parameters vary independently. A policy that degrades gracefully just outside its ranges does better than this; one that fails sharply does worse.

This is why teams randomize generously, and why a policy that can estimate where it is in parameter space (Section 4) beats one that has to be robust everywhere at once.

Section 2

Where simulators fall short

Every robot runs the same loop. The world moves, sensors measure it, perception turns measurements into a state estimate, a policy chooses an action, and actuators push on the world. A simulator replaces each box with a model and each arrow with an instant hand-off. The gap lives in both places: in each model’s errors, and in the time each hand-off actually takes on hardware.

Section 3

Why small gaps become big failures

Most individual gaps look small: a millimetre of calibration error, 40 milliseconds of delay, a 2% failure rate per step. They grow through three mechanisms.

Errors stack against a tolerance

A pick or an insertion succeeds when the gripper arrives within the task’s tolerance: the clearance, chamfer or compliance that absorbs error. Every link between camera and fingertip adds some error. In simulation most links are exact, so the budget is empty and success is 100%. On site, they all contribute.

Two kinds of error behave differently. Random error changes every attempt and widens the spread. Bias stays the same on a given cell and shifts the whole spread. It comes from calibration, kinematic error and deflection, and it differs from cell to cell. That is where deployment-to-deployment variation starts.

Look at the smallest number

Arm repeatability, the figure on the datasheet, barely registers. Calibration and perception dominate. Fixtured automation never needed those to be good, because it taught one pose and repeated it. A messy tote needs every link right at once.

Delay destabilizes fast loops

Field note · Tan et al. 2018

In the Bullet simulator, motor commands took effect instantly and sensors reported back at once. On the Minitaur robot the servo added about 3 ms and the locomotion controller about 15 to 19 ms. The team measured those delays and simulated them, alongside an improved actuator model.

In simulation the robot acts on the state it sees at the instant it sees it. On hardware the image is already tens of milliseconds old by the time the motors respond: exposure, readout, transfer, inference, bus. A controller tuned for speed in a zero-delay simulator has no margin left for that delay.

Field note · Peng et al. 2018

Training with a fixed time between actions, instead of a randomized one, cut real-robot success on their pushing task from 0.89 to 0.29.

Arms doing slow pick-and-place in position control tolerate delay well. Delay bites in fast or force-sensitive loops: visual servoing, contact and insertion, balancing, walking, anything near a person.

Long tasks multiply small failure rates

A task with many steps succeeds only if every step does. A simulator that is 2% optimistic per step is wildly optimistic over a 30-step task. Recovery changes the arithmetic: a failure the robot notices and retries costs time, not the task.

Simulators rarely teach recovery from the failures that happen on site, because they do not produce those failures. That is one more reason to bring real failures back into simulation (Section 5).

Section 4

Closing the gap

There are four software moves and one hardware move. Narrow the gap by measuring reality and making the simulator match (system identification). Cover it by training across ranges wide enough to include reality (domain randomization). Infer it online, letting the policy estimate hidden parameters from what it observes (adaptation). Close the remainder with real data. And widen the tolerance with hardware that forgives error: chamfers, compliance, suction, force control.

Field note · OpenAI 2019

The Rubik’s-cube hand was trained entirely in simulation with automatic domain randomization, which widened the ranges as the policy improved. On the real Shadow Hand it succeeded 60% of the time on scrambles needing 15 rotations and 20% on the hardest, needing 26.

Field note · Kumar et al. 2021

RMA trained a quadruped policy entirely in simulation, plus a module that estimates terrain and payload from the last fraction of a second of motion. Deployed without fine-tuning, the A1 walked on sand, mud, hiking trails, tall grass and a dirt pile with no failures in the authors’ trials.

The best-documented example measured the same policies in both worlds. Peng and colleagues trained a Fetch arm to push a puck to a target, entirely in simulation, then tested on the real arm.

What is changing

GPU-parallel simulators make it cheap to train across thousands of randomized worlds at once. Isaac Lab and MuJoCo Playground are widely used, and in 2025 NVIDIA, Google DeepMind and Disney Research announced Newton, an open physics engine now hosted by the Linux Foundation. That makes randomization cheaper. It does not add physics a simulator is missing. Learned world models, trained on video, are starting to cover appearance and variety, with no guarantee that their contact physics is right.

Section 5

Working once versus working every time

Crossing the gap once, on one robot, on one day, is a demo. Deployment means the same result across units, sites, shifts and months. That is a separate problem, with its own failure modes.

Accuracy and repeatability are different numbers

Standard · ISO 9283

Pose accuracy: the distance between the commanded pose and the mean of the poses actually reached. Pose repeatability: RP = l̄ + 3·Sl, the mean distance of the reached poses from their own centre plus three standard deviations of that distance.

Industrial standards separate two questions. Does the arm go where it was told, on average? That is accuracy. Does it go to the same place every time? That is repeatability.

Data · arm specs and calibration

Universal Robots rates the UR5e at ±0.03 mm pose repeatability per ISO 9283. In Nubiola Batlle’s 2014 calibration work at ÉTS Montréal, calibration cut an industrial arm’s mean distance error across its workspace from 1.062 mm to 0.400 mm.

Arms are built to be repeatable: a UR5e is rated at ±0.03 mm. Their absolute accuracy is far worse. An uncalibrated industrial arm is commonly off by around a millimetre and sometimes several, and careful calibration brings that down to a few tenths.

Why unstructured work is harder

Classical automation needs only repeatability. Teach a pose once, fixture the part, and the arm returns to it within hundredths of a millimetre for years. Unstructured work removes the fixture. Every target is computed from perception, so success depends on accuracy, which means every model in the loop being right at the same time. This is the sim2real gap showing up as a calibration problem, and it is why two identical cells can perform differently.

A fleet’s failures have structure

Run the same software on 12 cells at 3 sites for 30 days. The result is not one success rate. Failures cluster by unit, by site and day, and by time since the last calibration. Each pattern points to a different cause.

How many trials before a number means something

A success rate measured over a few trials is an interval, not a number. Many robot-learning results are reported over 10 to 50 real trials; the Peng et al. figures above used 10 to 28.

A deployment loop that crosses the gap on purpose

Teams that deploy repeatably treat the gap as something to measure all the time, rather than something to close once. Each cycle crosses it twice: failures travel from site to simulation as logs and scans, and fixes travel back as versioned releases.

Field note · Li et al. 2024

SIMPLER compared more than 1,500 paired simulated and real evaluations of manipulation policies, across two robots and eight task families, and found strong correlation once control and visual differences were matched.

Make the simulator earn its vote

A simulator is useful as a release gate only if it ranks changes the way reality does. Kadian et al. measured this for nine navigation models: simulated and real rankings correlated at 0.18 until they removed a wall-sliding shortcut agents had been exploiting, then 0.844. Track that correlation for your own tasks, and treat a drop as a bug in the simulator.
